An essay, in eleven parts

On the Keeping of Lists, Being a Short Account of Several Long Ones

In which the author attempts to organise a sock drawer, a life, and a small municipal library, and succeeds at roughly one of these

by Marguerite A. Pell

❦

Every list begins as a promise and ends as a confession. Mine began on the back of a gas bill, in pencil, under the heading Things To Do Before the Weekend, and it ended four years later as a three-ring binder with tabbed dividers, an index, and a short preface addressed to whoever might find it after I was gone.† I do not think this was the plan.

The trouble with lists is that they look finished. A column of items, each on its own line, has the confident air of a thing that knows where it is going. It is only when you try to cross something off that you discover how many of them were never really tasks at all, but wishes in a borrowed coat.

What follows, then, is an incomplete inventory of the lists I have kept, arranged in the order in which they defeated me:

  1. The list of books to read, which grows by three for every one finished.
  2. The list of people owed a letter, now mostly in the past tense.
  3. The list of lists, which was a mistake, and which I am still keeping.

It would be easy to conclude from this that lists are useless. That is not my conclusion. My conclusion, such as it is, comes at the end, which is where conclusions are traditionally kept, and you will have to read the other ten parts to reach it.

I tried, for a while, to keep my lists in a notebook with numbered pages, on the theory that a list with page numbers would be ashamed to wander. It wandered. By the spring it had reached page two hundred and twelve, by which time the early pages had acquired the faded authority of scripture and could no longer be amended.
